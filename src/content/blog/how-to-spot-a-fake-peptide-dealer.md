The peptide market on Telegram is mostly legitimate and entirely unregulated. That combination means the scam is always one introduction away, and it always looks the same: a clean channel, good prices, fast replies, and nothing you can verify.

Twelve checks. Most take seconds. A real dealer passes all of them without effort, because they're not doing anything unusual.

## The checks

**1. Does the handle have dated history going back months?** Scroll back. A channel three weeks old with 4,000 subscribers bought the subscribers. History is the cheapest thing to fake badly and the hardest to fake well.

**2. Are the vouch screenshots dated and consecutive?** One screenshot cropped nine ways is not a paper trail. You want different days, different buyers, visible timestamps.

**3. Do they send a COA before you pay?** Ask. A real dealer sends a PDF with a lab name and batch ID. A scammer sends a screenshot, or says "after payment," or sends someone else's certificate with a different batch on it.

**4. Does the batch ID on the COA match the batch you were quoted?** This is the single strongest check on the list. A COA for a different batch proves nothing about your vial.

**5. Is the lab name a real company?** Search it. If there's no website, no address and no analytical footprint, the certificate was made in Canva.

**6. Are prices inside a believable band?** 10mg tirzepatide at 30 € exists, but it's usually not tirzepatide at 98%. Wildly under-band means a different lab or a different molecule.

**7. Is there a written reship policy?** Seizure happens. A dealer with a policy has thought about it. A dealer without one has thought about not paying out.

**8. Do they take a payment rail you can reverse?** Card, or SEPA to a named business account. Crypto-only means irreversible by design — fine if everything else checks out, worse if it doesn't.

**9. Is the person identifiable?** A name, a face, a business, a location. Not a handle and a stock avatar. Anonymity is convenient for the dealer and expensive for you.

**10. Do they answer a technical question properly?** Ask what the purity method on the COA is, or the theoretical mass of the peptide. A reseller can read it off the certificate. A scammer has to bluff.

**11. Is the channel full of broker ads?** Every fifth post selling a prop firm or a crypto signal means the peptides are a side business. Fine sometimes, a warning when it's most of the feed.

**12. Do they push you to order fast?** Batch going, price rising tomorrow, limited stock. Real deadlines exist; manufactured ones are a pressure tactic.

## The three that catch most scams

If you only run three, run 3, 4 and 6. Certificate before payment, batch ID match, believable price.

A scammer can fake a channel, fake subscribers and fake vouches in an afternoon. Producing a coherent PDF with a real lab and a matching batch number takes actual work — which is exactly why most don't.

## Red flags that aren't on the list

Not every annoyance is fraud, but these correlate:

- Prices only in DM, never in public
- "DM me for the list" with no catalogue
- Photos of vials with no label visible
- A "manager" who appears only after you've messaged the main handle
- Payment to a personal name in a different country than the business claims
- Shipping "from within the EU" with no EU return address

## What to do if you've already paid a fake

Most of it is unrecoverable. Do three things: chargeback on card if the window is open, report the handle to Telegram, and post the batch ID and the handle in a public peptide group so the next buyer finds it.

## What a legitimate dealer looks like

Four years of visible history. A COA on request, before payment, with a real lab and a matching batch. Prices inside the band. A written reship policy. A named person. Answers to technical questions that don't require a pause.

None of that is extraordinary. It's the baseline, and the baseline is enough to filter out nearly everything.
