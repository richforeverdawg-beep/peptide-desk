A dry vial and a mixed vial are two different storage problems, and the rules that protect one can destroy the other.

Get this right and a peptide keeps for months. Get it wrong and it degrades in days with no visible sign until the solution clouds.

## The two states

**Lyophilised powder** — a freeze-dried cake. Low water activity, so hydrolysis is near-stopped. Stable for months, and the main risks are heat, light and moisture ingress.

**Reconstituted solution** — water back in, hydrolysis running, bacterial growth possible. Weeks at best, refrigerated, and no tolerance for freezing.

Everything below follows from that split.

## Dry powder

Store at 2–8°C for anything you'll use within a few months. For long-term storage, −20°C is better, and the vial should be at room temperature before you open it so condensation doesn't form on the cake.

- Keep it dark. Amber vials or a closed box; UV degrades aromatic residues.
- Keep it dry. A silica sachet in the storage box is cheap and does more than most people expect.
- Don't freeze-thaw dry vials either. Repeated cycling draws moisture in.
- Never store powder in a fridge door — the temperature swings every time it opens.

## Reconstituted solution

Refrigerate at 2–8°C, upright, protected from light, and use within four to six weeks. Some peptides are shorter than that; GHK-Cu and anything copper-bearing is less stable in solution.

- **Never freeze a solution.** Ice crystals shear the peptide, and freeze-thaw cycling does it repeatedly.
- Swab the stopper before every draw. A multi-dose vial gets punctured dozens of times.
- Keep it upright so the solution sits off the stopper.
- Write the mix date on the cap. Four weeks from that date, stop.

## The visible and invisible failures

Visible: cloudiness, particles, colour change, a gel that won't clear, a cake that collapsed into a sticky mass before you opened it.

Invisible: deamidation, oxidation, aggregation, sequence truncation. None of these change how the vial looks. A clear solution can be substantially degraded, which is why the date matters more than your eyes.

Aggregation is the one to care about most — clumped peptide is not just weaker, it's the fraction most likely to provoke a reaction.

## Shipping and the cold chain

Powder tolerates transit. A vial that sat in a warm van for two days is usually fine. A vial that sat in direct sun is not, and a collapsed cake or a yellowed one is the evidence.

A solution shipped cold and arrived warm has been through a full cycle. If a pre-mixed vial arrives warm, treat its clock as already partly spent.

## Humidity

Water is the enemy of a dry cake. Opening a vial from the freezer straight into a warm room puts condensation on the powder before the water goes in — and you've added an unmeasured volume.

Let frozen vials sit sealed for thirty minutes. Don't wipe the inside of the vial with anything.

## Light

Most peptides are light-sensitive to some degree. Amber glass exists for a reason. A closed drawer is fine; a windowsill is not.

## The practical routine

1. Dry vials in a closed box, in the fridge, away from the door.
2. Long-term stock in the freezer, sealed, in a box.
3. Room temperature for thirty minutes before reconstituting.
4. Mix date on the cap, in permanent marker.
5. Upright in the fridge once mixed.
6. Four weeks, then replace.

## Where people go wrong

**"The fridge is the safest place for everything."** It is, for both states — but the fridge is not a freezer, and the door shelf is not the fridge.

**Freezing a mixed vial "to make it last longer."** It shortens it, unless you're aliquotting single doses into separate vials first, which almost nobody does.

**Storing in the bathroom.** Warm, humid, and the worst room in the house for a hygroscopic powder.

**Judging by appearance.** Clarity is not potency.

**Hoarding.** A large order at a good price is only a saving if it's used inside the shelf life. Twenty vials you can't get through in four months is not a bargain, it's a fridge full of decaying powder.
