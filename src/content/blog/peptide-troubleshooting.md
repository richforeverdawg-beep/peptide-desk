Five things go wrong with a vial and four of them have the same three causes. This is the diagnostic order, and what each outcome actually means.

None of it is mysterious once the causes are laid out.

## First: what state is the vial in?

Before anything else, establish that.

- **Still powder, cake looks wrong** → storage or transit problem, before you touched it
- **Mixed and cloudy** → undissolved solids or precipitation
- **Mixed and clear, then went cloudy** → pH or degradation
- **Gelled** → concentration or shear
- **Clear and fine, but stings** → the injection site or the water, not the peptide

Different states, different answers. Don't skip this step.

## Cloudy after mixing

**Cause 1: you sprayed the water onto the cake.** Powder stuck to the shoulder, foam, and solids that never went in. It'll clear with time and swirling, but the concentration is now lower than you calculated.

**Cause 2: the cake was warm or heat-damaged in transit.** A collapsed, sticky or yellowed cake is the tell before you even add water. This one doesn't clear. Note the batch and tell the dealer.

**Cause 3: the water.** Wrong water, or a contaminated source. Use bacteriostatic water, fresh bottle.

Fix: swirl for twenty minutes. If it clears, use it and adjust your expectations on concentration. If it doesn't, and the cake looked wrong beforehand, it's the batch.

## Won't dissolve at all

Same three causes, plus one: some peptides are simply slow. GHK-Cu and the larger fragments can take half an hour.

Fix: twenty minutes of intermittent swirling at room temperature. No heat, no stirring with the needle, no shaking. If there's still visible solid at forty minutes, stop adding effort and note the batch.

## Gelled

A gel means the peptide is too concentrated for the water volume, or it's been sheared.

Fix: it usually re-dissolves with more swirling and time. If not, add a small measured volume of water and recalculate the concentration from the new total — write the new number on the cap, because the old one is now wrong.

## Clear, then cloudy

This is the one to take seriously. A solution that clears and then precipitates has probably shifted pH or started aggregating.

Fix: try a fresh bottle of water first, since that's the cheap variable. If it clouds again, the vial is compromised — finish it quickly rather than over weeks, and don't draw from it for a dose you care about.

## Stings or burns on injection

Usually not the peptide.

- **Cold solution straight from the fridge** — let the vial reach room temperature
- **Alcohol still wet on the skin** — let the swab dry
- **Bacteriostatic water with a high benzyl alcohol content** — some brands are harsher than others
- **Injection too fast** — push slowly
- **Site too shallow or too close to a nerve** — rotate sites

Benzyl alcohol genuinely does sting. If the same peptide in the same water stung yesterday and stings today, it's the water or the technique, not the batch.

## A lump or a red patch

Small, firm, fading in a few days: normal, usually a shallow injection or too much volume in one depot. Keep the volume per site small and rotate.

Spreading redness, heat, or a lump that grows past three days: possibly a reaction to the batch rather than technique. Stop, note the batch ID, photograph it, tell the dealer.

## Colour change

Some peptides carry colour — GHK-Cu is blue, and a green tint as it oxidises is expected. Anything else turning yellow or brown is degrading.

## What is never worth salvaging

- Visible particles that don't clear
- A cake that arrived collapsed and won't dissolve
- Solution that clouded twice after two fresh water bottles
- Anything with a batch ID that doesn't match its COA

Four vials' worth of peptide is cheaper than a bad week.

## The prevention list

1. Aim water at the glass, not the cake
2. Swirl, never shake
3. Bacteriostatic water, fresh
4. Never freeze a solution
5. Room temperature before injecting
6. Small volumes per site, rotated
7. Fridge upright, four weeks maximum

Almost every entry above is one of those seven done wrong.
